## 0. Assumptions and modelling choices

### Taken from the assignment (not choices)

* Risk-neutral GBM between dividend dates: $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$, with $K=100$, $T=3/4$, $r=\log 1.05$, $\sigma=0.3$.
* Proportional dividends at $d_k\in\{5/24,11/24,17/24\}$: $S_{d_k^+}=(1-\delta)S_{d_k^-}$. The strike is not adjusted, and the put holder does not receive the dividend.
* Exercise is allowed only at $t_j=jT/N$, including $t_0$. At a dividend date the jump is applied first, and then the exercise decision is made. Every surviving put is settled at $T$.
* $V_\delta(d_k,s)$ and $b_\delta(d_k)$ refer to the post-jump state. $b_\delta(T)=K$.

### Our assumptions (please check them)

| # | Assumption | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | "Exercise just before / just after the jump" means two instants with **zero elapsed time** between them, so no discounting and no diffusion happens between $d_k^-$ and $d_k^+$. | This is the standard meaning of $d_k^\pm$ and what makes the identities in items 1 and 4 exact. |
| A2 | For item 2 in continuous time, $t=d_k-\varepsilon$ lies strictly after $d_{k-1}$, so there is no dividend in $(t,d_k)$. | The assignment states this. It is what gives $\mathbb E[S_{d_k^-}\mid S_t=s]=se^{r\varepsilon}$. |

### Our own numerical reference (modelling choices)

These choices are ours and are only for illustrating Part 1.

* **What it solves.** Backward induction on a uniform grid in $x=\log S$, centred so that $S=K$ is a node. That puts the payoff kink exactly on a node.
* **Continuation value.** The one-step Gaussian expectation is computed **exactly** for the piecewise-linear interpolant of $V$ (fixed "hat" weights, applied as a correlation). There is no time-stepping error.
* **Variance correction.** Integrating the hat basis exactly adds a spurious variance of $dx^2/6$ per step. We use $\sigma^2h-dx^2/6$ in the kernel. Without this, the European check error grew with $N$: about $2.5\times10^{-3}$ at $N=180$ and $5\times10^{-3}$ at $N=360$ (at $S_0=100$). With it, the error is about $2\times10^{-5}$ at both $N$.
* **Grid spacing and the dividend.** We set $dx=|\log(1-0.0125)|/10$, so a dividend moves the price by exactly 10 grid nodes. There is no interpolation error at the jump. Both values of $\delta$ use the same grid, so any difference between them comes from the model, not the grid.
* **Truncated domain.** The grid covers $S\in[Ke^{-7},Ke^{3}]$. Values outside it are extended by the edge value. The only artefact we saw was at the lowest 10 nodes, where the dividend shift reaches off the grid; those nodes are excluded from the item-1 check.

**Validation** (`validate_solver.py`):

* The European put and call both match the closed form ($S_0(1-\delta)^3$ in Black–Scholes) to about $2\times10^{-5}$.
* Halving $dx$ changes the American values by about $10^{-5}$ and the boundary by about $10^{-3}$ dollars.

These accuracy figures come from our own checks, not from an independent source. Treat them as approximate.

## Item 1 — $V_\delta(d_k^-,s)=V_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)$

Just before the jump, the holder of an in-the-money put has two choices.

* **Exercise now.** This pays $K-s$.
* **Hold through the jump.** This is worth $V_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)$. Nothing random happens between $d_k^-$ and $d_k^+$ (A1), and the put does not receive the dividend, so the only effect of the jump is to move the state to $(1-\delta)s$.

So in general $V_\delta(d_k^-,s)=\max\{(K-s)^+,\,V_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)\}$.

The option to exercise immediately after the jump is always available. Therefore

$$
V_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)\;\ge\;K-(1-\delta)s\;=\;(K-s)+\delta s\;>\;K-s\qquad(0<s<K).
$$

For $s\ge K$ the payoff is zero, and $V\ge0$ holds trivially. So the maximum is always attained by holding, and the identity follows.

**Before vs after.** For an in-the-money put:

* Exercising immediately after the jump pays $K-(1-\delta)s$.
* Exercising immediately before pays $K-s$.

The difference is $\delta s>0$, and it comes with **no loss of interest**, because no time passes. Pre-jump exercise is strictly dominated. That is why a put implementation may apply the jump first and then decide.

**Experiment E1.** At each $d_k$ ($N=180$, $\delta=0.0125$), on every grid node with $0<s<K$:

* $\min_s[V(d_k^-,s)-(K-s)]=1.15\times10^{-3}>0$ (at the smallest $s$ on the grid).
* $\min_s[V(d_k^-,s)-(K-s)-\delta s]\approx-4\times10^{-14}$, i.e. zero to machine precision.
* No grid node has pre-jump exercise optimal.

For $s\lesssim 90$ the gap equals $\delta s$ exactly (Fig. `p1_pre_jump`, left panel). The reason is that $(1-\delta)s$ is then below the post-dividend boundary $b(d_3)\approx0.893K$, so the put is exercised at $d_3^+$.

We also solved the put under three orderings. Allowing exercise on **both** sides of the jump gives **exactly** the same values as "jump then exercise" (max difference 0). Allowing exercise **only before** the jump is worse: $V(0,100)=10.1201$ vs $10.1264$.

## Item 2 — the dividend cap

**Lower bound.** Take $t=d_k-\varepsilon$ with no dividend in $(t,d_k)$ (A2). A feasible strategy is: wait until $d_k^+$, then exercise. Using $(x)^+\ge x$ and $\mathbb E[S_{d_k^-}\mid S_t=s]=se^{r\varepsilon}$:

$$
V_\delta(t,s)\ \ge\ e^{-r\varepsilon}\,\mathbb E\big[(K-(1-\delta)S_{d_k^-})^+\big]
\ \ge\ e^{-r\varepsilon}\big(K-(1-\delta)se^{r\varepsilon}\big)=Ke^{-r\varepsilon}-(1-\delta)s .
$$

**Cap.** Suppose $s$ is in the exercise region, so $V=K-s$. Then

$$
K-s\ \ge\ Ke^{-r\varepsilon}-(1-\delta)s\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s\le \frac{K(1-e^{-r\varepsilon})}{\delta}.
$$

Taking the supremum (with the $\{0\}$ convention) gives

$$
b_\delta(d_k-\varepsilon)\le K(1-e^{-r\varepsilon})/\delta .
$$

**Limit.** The right-hand side is about $Kr\varepsilon/\delta$ and tends to 0. Since $b\ge0$, $\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}b_\delta(d_k-\varepsilon)=0$.

**Grid version.** In the grid problem, $d_k=t_{j_d}$ is an exercise date, so "stop at $t_{j_d}$, after the jump" is an admissible grid stopping time from $t_j<t_{j_d}$. The same argument holds with $\varepsilon=(j_d-j)h$. **E2 check:** our solver satisfies $V\ge Ke^{-r\varepsilon}-(1-\delta)s$ on every grid date and node, with worst slack about $+9\times10^{-13}$.

**How close is "close enough" depends on $s$.** Fix $0<s<K$. The dividend benefit $\delta s$ beats the interest cost $K(1-e^{-r\varepsilon})$ whenever

$$
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^*(s)=-\tfrac1r\log(1-\delta s/K)\approx \delta s/(rK).
$$

Inside that window, $s$ is never exercised. So $\varepsilon^*$ is proportional to $s$: small prices need to be much closer to the dividend.

| $s$ | $\varepsilon^*$ (days) | grid steps, $N=180$ | grid steps, $N=360$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 9.4 | 6.2 | 12.3 |
| 40 | 37.5 | 24.7 | 49.3 |
| 80 | 75.2 | 49.4 | 98.9 |

The bound makes this precise because it depends on $s$ and $\varepsilon$ only through $\delta s$ versus $K(1-e^{-r\varepsilon})$.

**Positive boundary at a finite grid distance.** One step before a dividend the cap is $K(1-e^{-rh})/\delta$:

* 1.626 for $N=180$
* 0.813 for $N=360$

These values are positive, and they are exactly what our solver produces at $j_d-1$. This does not contradict a zero limit. The limit concerns $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, and on a grid $\varepsilon\ge h$. As $h\to0$ the one-step cap shrinks linearly in $h$.

**An observation from the experiment (not required, but it explains the C&R picture).** Close to each dividend, the numerical boundary lies **on** the cap to about $10^{-6}$:

* $N=180$: for about 31–37 grid steps
* $N=360$: for about 60 grid steps

(tolerance $|b/\text{cap}-1|<10^{-3}$). The boundary leaves the cap at about $0.49$–$0.60\,K$.

The reason: for $s$ well below the post-dividend boundary, exercise at $d_k^+$ is almost certain. The continuation value then *equals* the wait-and-exercise value, so the inequality is tight. This is why the Cox–Rubinstein figure shows **straight lines** with slope $\approx rK/\delta\approx3.9K$ per year of $u$. The lines bend over once there is a real chance of ending above the post-jump boundary.

**Upward jump in calendar time.** Just before $d_k$ the boundary is near 0. At $d_k$ (post-jump) it jumps up to the post-dividend level. In our solver ($N=180$) that level is:

* $0.686K$ at $d_1$
* $0.746K$ at $d_2$
* $0.893K$ at $d_3$

Right after the jump, the next dividend is far away (or there is none), so the incentive to wait has gone. **[CITE C&R: discussion of the exercise boundary around Fig. 5-37.]**

**Limit at maturity.** After $d_3$ there is no dividend left and $r>0$, so the boundary rises to $K$ as $t\to T$. This is the standard no-dividend American put property **[CITE C&R]**. On our grid, $b_{N-1}/K=0.963$ ($N=180$) and $0.972$ ($N=360$), with $b_N=K$ by definition.

## Item 3 — dividend ordering

**Coupling.** Fix $N$, $t_j$ and $s$. Use **one** Brownian motion $W$ on $[t_j,T]$. For $i\ge j$:

$$
S^0_i=s\,e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)(t_i-t_j)+\sigma(W_{t_i}-W_{t_j})},\qquad S^\delta_i=S^0_i(1-\delta)^{n_{ji}},
$$

where $n_{ji}$ is the number of dividend dates in $(t_j,t_i]$. The start $t_j$ is excluded, because a start at a dividend date is post-jump. Each model on its own has the correct law, so values computed from the coupling are the model values. Also $0\le(1-\delta)^{n}\le1$, so $S^\delta_i\le S^0_i$ on every path.

**Common stopping times.** Both price processes are one-to-one functions of the same increments, so they generate the same filtration (A3). The set $\mathcal T$ of grid stopping times is therefore the same for both models. For any $\tau\in\mathcal T$, the map $x\mapsto(K-x)^+$ is non-increasing, so pathwise

$$
e^{-r(\tau-t_j)}(K-S^\delta_\tau)^+\ \ge\ e^{-r(\tau-t_j)}(K-S^0_\tau)^+ .
$$

Taking expectations gives $J_\delta(\tau)\ge J_0(\tau)$. Then $V_{\delta,N}(t_j,s)\ge J_\delta(\tau)\ge J_0(\tau)$ for every $\tau$. Taking the supremum over $\tau$:

$$
V_{0.0125,N}(t_j,s)\ge V_{0,N}(t_j,s).
$$

**Boundaries.** Suppose $s<K$ is in the $\delta$-exercise region, so $V_\delta(t_j,s)=K-s$. Then

$$
K-s=V_\delta\ge V_0\ge K-s ,
$$

where the last inequality holds because $\tau=t_j$ is admissible. So $V_0(t_j,s)=K-s$. This shows $E_\delta(t_j)\subseteq E_0(t_j)$. Taking suprema, $b_{0.0125,N}(t_j)\le b_{0,N}(t_j)$. This argument does not need the exercise regions to be intervals.

**Equality for $t_j\ge d_3$.** There is no dividend in $(t_j,T]$, so $n_{ji}\equiv0$ and $S^\delta\equiv S^0$ pathwise. The two problems are identical, so the values and boundaries agree. **At $t_j=d_3$:** both models are evaluated at the same **post-jump** price $s$. The $\delta$-model's last dividend has already been paid and is inside $s$. The future dynamics are identical, so $V_\delta(d_3,s)=V_0(d_3,s)$.

**Experiment E3 (our solver as "ref"):**

| $N$ | $j_*$ | $A$ | $B$ | $F$ | $\max_s(V_0-V_\delta)^+$ at $t_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 180 | 170 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 360 | 340 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

$F$ is exactly 0 because after $j_*$ the two computations are identical floating-point operations. The value ordering also holds on **every** date $j$, not only $t_0$.

**Coupled Monte Carlo** (20,000 paths, $N=180$, $S=100$, our seed 9001, outside the assignment's seed ranges):

| Stopping rule | Mean of $Q_\delta-Q_0$ | Paths with $Q_\delta<Q_0$ |
|---|---|---|
| One common $\tau$ from shared information: first hit of $b^0_{\text{ref}}$ by $S^0$ | $+1.081$ (s.e. 0.008) | **0%** |
| Each model stops by its own rule: $S\le0.8K$ applied to its own price | $+1.001$ (s.e. 0.020) | 12.4% |

The second row shows why the proof fixes a **common** $\tau$ before comparing payoffs. With model-specific stopping times, pathwise dominance fails on some paths, even though the ordering of the optimal values still holds.

## Item 4 — exercise timing for a call

The call's intrinsic value **falls** at the jump: $((1-\delta)s-K)^+\le(s-K)^+$. The call holder does not get the dividend, but exercising just before the jump delivers the cum-dividend stock and so captures it. Just before the jump the holder chooses between:

* **Exercise now:** $(s-K)^+$.
* **Hold through the jump:** $C_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)$.

So

$$
C_\delta(d_k^-,s)=\max\{(s-K)^+,\ C_\delta(d_k^+,(1-\delta)s)\}.
$$

Unlike the put, this maximum is **not redundant**. For large $s$, exercising gives $s-K$, while holding is worth roughly $(1-\delta)s-Ke^{-r(T-d_k)}$ plus a small time value. The first is larger once $\delta s$ is big enough.

**What an implementation must do.** A call implementation must test exercise **before** applying the dividend at $d_k$:

$$
W(s)=\max\{\text{payoff}(s),\ V^+((1-\delta)s)\}.
$$

For the put, item 1 shows this pre-jump option is worthless, so "jump, then decide" is exact. The two identities have the same structure, $\max\{\text{pre-jump payoff},\ \text{post-jump value}\}$. They differ only in which term can win.

**Experiment E4** ($N=180$, $\delta=0.0125$):

| Ordering | $C(0,80)$ | $C(0,100)$ | $C(0,120)$ | $C(0,140)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| European | 2.3918 | 9.9037 | 23.0661 | 39.7760 |
| Put-style (jump, then decide) | 2.5183 | 10.4029 | 24.2031 | 41.8020 |
| Exercise pre-jump only | 2.5251 | 10.4198 | 24.2278 | 41.8287 |
| **Both (correct)** | **2.5251** | **10.4198** | **24.2278** | **41.8287** |

* **Pre-jump exercise thresholds** (correct call): $s^*=139.7$ at $d_1$, $128.8$ at $d_2$, $104.8$ at $d_3$.
* **No exercise at any other date** (checked on the grid for $S<5K$).
* **The put-style call** still exercises, but **one grid step early**, at $t_{j_d-1}$ (threshold 105.2 before $d_3$). It gives up interest $K(1-e^{-rh})$, so its value lies between European and correct. The error shrinks as $h\to0$.

For the put, "both" and "after" orderings agree exactly (item 1).
